## Source [^1]
- Given two distinct, odd primes $p$ and $q$, then $p$ is a quadratic residue modulo $q$ if and only if $q$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$, unless $p \equiv q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, in which case precisely one of $p, q$ is a quadratic residue modulo the other. This was proved by Gauss around 1801 but had been suspected since Euler’s time. As an application, note that it is not immediately obvious whether $x^2 \equiv 31 \pmod{257}$ has a solution, but now note, in terms of the Legendre symbol, that $(31|257) = (257|31) = (9|31) = 1$ as 9 is clearly a quadratic residue and so 31 is a quadratic residue modulo 257. The theorem does not provide guidance on what the two square roots are—in this case $\pm 51$.
