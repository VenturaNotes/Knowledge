## Source [^1]
- (terminal velocity) When an object falls to Earth from a great height, its speed, in certain mathematical models, tends to a value called its terminal speed. One possible mathematical model gives the equation $m\mathbf{\ddot{r}} = -mg\mathbf{j} - c\mathbf{\dot{r}}$, where $m$ is the mass of the particle and $\mathbf{j}$ the unit vector in the direction vertically upwards. The second term on the right-hand side, in which $c$ is a positive constant, arises from the air resistance. The velocity corresponding to $\mathbf{\dot{r}} = 0$ is equal to $(-mg/c)\mathbf{j}$, which is called the terminal velocity. The terminal speed is the magnitude $mg/c$ of this velocity. If, instead, the equation $m\mathbf{\ddot{r}} = -mg\mathbf{j} - c|\mathbf{\dot{r}}|\mathbf{\dot{r}}$ is used, the terminal speed is $\sqrt{mg/c}$ and the terminal velocity is $-\sqrt{mg/c}\mathbf{j}$. As the particle falls, its velocity tends to the terminal velocity as $t$ increases, irrespective of the initial conditions.
