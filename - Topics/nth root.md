## Source [^1]
- For any real number $a$, an $n$th root of $a$ is a number $x$ such that $x^n = a$. When $n = 2$, it is called a square root, and when $n = 3$ a cube root.
- First consider $n$ even. If $a < 0$, there is no real number $x$ such that $x^n = a$. If $a > 0$, there are two such numbers, one positive and one negative. For $a \geq 0$, the notation $\sqrt[n]{a}$ is used to denote quite specifically the non-negative $n$th root of $a$. For example, $\sqrt[4]{16} = 2$, and 16 has two real fourth roots, namely 2 and $-2$.
- Next consider $n$ odd. For all values of $a$, there is a unique number $x$ such that $x^n = a$, and it is denoted by an $\sqrt[n]{a}$. For example, $\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2$.
- For a non-zero complex number $a$, there are $n$ complex numbers $z$ such that $z^n = a$, which may also be referred to as $n$th roots of $a$. In the Argand diagram these $n$th roots form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.
