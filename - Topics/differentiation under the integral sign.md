- This states that$$\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\alpha} \int_a^b f(t, \alpha) \text{d}t = \int_a^b \frac{\partial f}{\partial \alpha}(t, \alpha) \text{d}t.$$
- This is a special case of \*Leibniz's integral rule. As an example, for $\alpha > 0$ define$$I(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x} - e^{-\alpha x}}{x} \text{d}x.$$
- Then$$I'(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty \frac{xe^{-\alpha x}}{x} \text{d}x = \int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha x} \text{d}x = \frac{1}{\alpha},$$and integrating gives $I(\alpha) = c + \ln \alpha$ for some constant $c$. As $I(1) = 0$ then $I(\alpha) = \ln \alpha$.
