## Source [^1]
- The statement that $f(x)$ tends to $l$ as $x$ tends to $a$ from the left can be written: $f(x) \to l$ as $x \to a^-$. Another way of writing this is$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = l.$$
- The formal definition says that this is so if, given $\varepsilon > 0$, there is a number $\delta > 0$ such that, for all $x$ strictly between $a - \delta$ and $a, f(x)$ lies between $l - \varepsilon$ and $l + \varepsilon$. In place of $x \to a^-$, some authors use $x \nearrow a$. In the same way, the statement that $f(x)$ tends to $l$ as $x$ tends to $a$ from the right can be written: $f(x) \to l$ as $x \to a^+$. Another way of writing this is$$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = l.$$
- The formal definition says that this is so if, given $\varepsilon > 0$, there is a number $\delta > 0$ such that, for all $x$ strictly between $a$ and $a + \delta, f(x)$ lies between $l - \varepsilon$ and $l + \varepsilon$. In place of $x \to a^+$, some authors use $x \searrow a$. For example, if $f(x) = x - [x]$, then$$\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1, \quad \lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 0.$$
